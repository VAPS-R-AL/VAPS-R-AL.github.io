Continue, Abort, or Fall

Viability-Aware Policy Selection (VAPS) for Safe Humanoid Acrobatics

Abstract

Dynamic humanoid motions such as flips risk hardware damage, due to suboptimal policies, disturbances or sim-to-real gaps. A motion-tracking policy offers no way out once the maneuver leaves its reference, and a backup policy needs to take over to protect the hardware for a least-damage landing. Which backup to use matters as much as when to switch.

We present Viability-Aware Policy Selection (VAPS), which treats safety as a policy-conditioned, receding-horizon decision. Besides a protective-fall policy, we also train an abort policy which can abort the motion at any time, landing on its feet. At every control step, learned predictors estimate whether the nominal tracking policy and the abort policy remain viable over a short horizon, and a least-sacrificial hierarchy keeps the most task-ambitious behavior that remains viable.

In simulation with randomized disturbances, VAPS sharply reduces head contact and hand contact, which are the dominant sources of hardware damage; on the LimX Oli, we validate the viability predictors and the full VAPS controller for side-flip motions, with the whole stack running on the robot's own CPU. VAPS Pareto-dominates the strongest single-network alternatives we could train, including an end-to-end safe-tracking policy and students distilled from VAPS's own oracle-routed decisions, on the task-success/head-impact frontier. We also show that VAPS is a powerful framework to supervise undertrained policies and protect the hardware.

Method

Why a single switch is too coarse

  • Acrobatics are learned as reference-tracking policies, and training ends the episode the moment the robot leaves the reference — so nothing is learned about what follows.
  • Most safety mechanisms then offer one binary choice: continue, or trigger a single backup.
  • A flip lasts one to two seconds. There is no time left to correct or recover, only to abort safely.
  • Depending on the severity, the robot may still land on its feet rather than fall — and at these speeds even a learned safe fall can do serious damage.
  • The gap is the intermediate regime: the maneuver is already lost, a controlled landing is not.

Three specialists, each trained where it is entered

  • Nominal whichever tracking policy is deployed. Never trained on what follows a deviation, so it cannot serve as its own backup.
  • Abort seeks an upright base with only the feet in contact, trained from switch states snapshotted under disturbed nominal rollouts.
  • Protective fall shields head and hands once a fall is unavoidable; its success means mitigated harm, not task completion.

Policy-conditioned, receding-horizon viability

  • One predictor per candidate, re-estimated at every control step: does this policy fail within the next H steps?
  • Failure is a physical fall — non-foot terrain contact — not the tracking-error termination, which fires on deviations the robot often survives. The same predicate for both policies puts the two viabilities on one scale.
  • Each predictor reads a trailing window of its own policy's observations; the instantaneous state cannot tell a recoverable configuration from one already committed to failure.
  • Rollouts start from the states where that policy is actually entered — matching them moved the switching decision more than architecture, history length and sensing combined.
  • Positives are down-weighted by their time to failure (γ), ranking imminent failures above distant ones without changing the class balance.

The least-sacrificial rule

  • The ladder: the nominal completes the maneuver, the abort gives it up but keeps the robot upright, the protective fall gives up staying upright and only limits the damage.
  • Each step down costs more of the task and exposes the hardware to more risk, so the robot descends only as far as predicted viability forces it.
  • The executed policy is the least sacrificial one still predicted viable:
continue · πnom while the nominal policy is predicted viable, V̂nom > τnom
abort · πabort once the nominal alarms and the abort is still predicted viable, V̂nom ≤ τnom  ∧  V̂abort > τabort
fall · πprotect otherwise.
  • Both thresholds are set empirically: τnom trades false alarms against lead time, and is fixed at a false-alarm rate of at most 1% on held-out stable episodes (τnom = 0.2, τabort = 0.87).
  • The rule reacts to predicted viability, not to the cause of its loss.
  • One hierarchy therefore covers a mature policy under disturbance, an untested policy on hardware, and a stop demanded from outside the robot by an operator or a workspace monitor.

One real state, three futures

  • Counterfactual rollouts separate the maneuver has failed from recovery is no longer possible — and they are what the predictors are trained on.
  • Save a state, replay it under every candidate: the outcomes differ only in which policy ran next.
  • Here with a real trial — on the LimX Oli the nominal predictor called the switch mid-flight.
  • The robot's recorded state at that instant was re-run in simulation under each candidate.

Results in simulation

The backups are preferable over different bands of severity

  • At the training scale all three succeed without touching the ground.
  • At the intermediate scale the nominal starts to lose the maneuver, while the abort still returns nine in ten to a stance and the protective fall keeps the head clear on essentially every state it is handed.
  • At the strongest scale the nominal completes one episode in seven and puts its head down in five of eight; the abort still recovers three in five.
  • The maneuver becomes unrecoverable long before safe recovery does.

Predicting viability, not the final outcome

  • Baselines are SafeFall-style final-outcome predictors, retrained on our rollouts with the same splits and the same ≤1% false-alarm rule.
  • At the same episode-level false-alarm rate, VAPS detects more falls and warns about 60% earlier.
  • Pushing the baseline's label window out buys lead time only to t2 = 500 ms; past that, lead time and detection both regress.
  • A final-outcome label can only fire once the outcome is settled — often past the point where an upright landing is still reachable.
  • In the sweeps the horizon is decisive (median lead more than doubles from H = 10 to 50); the encoder barely matters.

  • But an abort that fails arrives in whatever attitude it lost control in: median peak head force 834 N against 540 N unprotected, and the highest head contact of any switching row.
  • The protective fall keeps the head clear (2.0% head contact, median peak head force zero) but is committed to the ground, so it falls more often than the nominal it supervises.
  • Routing keeps both. One-shot: head contact stays at 2.2%, within 0.2 points of always protecting, while the fall rate drops 14.2 points to 26.2%.
  • Escalating a failing abort to the protective fall removes the rest — head contact 1.6%, below the protective-fall ablation, with the mean peak head force among fallen episodes more than halved.
  • Fall rate and motion success are unchanged; supervision costs 2.5 points of motion success against the unsupervised nominal.

VAPS versus monolithic policies

Can a single network replace the structure?

  • Single-network RL — the protective specialist's damage terms folded into the task reward with weight λ, tracking terminations removed, warm-started from the nominal, λ swept at five seeds each.
  • Distilled students — copied from a teacher at least as good as VAPS: at every alarmed state, counterfactual rollouts pick the behavior that succeeds.
  • Two arms tilt the student toward protection (+ protect, + DAgger); two more add memory (a five-frame stack, a GRU) to rule out missing information rather than missing structure.

What the frontier shows

  • VAPS runs along the bottom edge: from about 55% success at 2% head contact to 62% at 4.6%.
  • The end-to-end policies never leave the nominal's corner — at most four points of head contact, before the weight breaks training and success scatters.
  • The students descend, then plateau near 10%. Architecture does not move that plateau; the training data does.
  • The DAgger shard takes the memoryless student from 17.9% to 10.1% and leaves the recurrent one where it was — its segments begin at the alarm, with no history for a hidden state to carry.
  • They learned how to protect the head, not when protecting is right: episodes ending upright without completing the maneuver run to fourteen points for VAPS and low single digits for the students.
Folding the specialists into one network preserves the skills but loses the decision of how much of the task to give up — exactly the part the explicit structure keeps inspectable.

The backups transfer

  • With a well-trained nominal policy, the states at which a switch actually occurs fall overwhelmingly where a controlled landing is still viable.
  • Triggering the switch manually mid-motion, the abort returned the robot to a stance in 17 of 19 trials.
  • The protective fall, handed the same maneuver, kept head and hands clear in 12 of 16 trials.

The whole hierarchy, closed loop on the robot

  • In the trials above the handover is commanded by an operator. Here the stack runs on the robot and every switch is the selector's own.
  • τnom was raised to 0.3 for this evaluation, to induce more frequent handoffs to the backups than the deployed operating point would.
  • Across 18 trials the controller produced four routing patterns, and the selected specialist met its objective in 15 of 18 — 83.3%, including trials with externally introduced disturbances.

What an unsupervised failure costs

People in the raw laboratory footage are obscured throughout: the two failure clips are composited with the robot sharp over a blurred background, the predictor trials carry a face pass, and the closed-loop trial keeps a feathered patch at the edge of frame.

Supervising new policies

  • VAPS is trained on a large dataset of randomized, perturbed simulation, so the same pipeline generalizes across policies — a protective framework for a new policy, or a new robot.
  • The test: the nominal policy retrained from scratch, a checkpoint every 2,000 iterations, VAPS deployed on all 16 from untrained to converged.
  • The abort and protective-fall policies are reused; only the nominal predictor is retrained.
  • Head contact under VAPS stays flat near the floor, while the unprotected policy ranges from 30 to 99%.
  • The two completion curves lie almost on top of each other — protection at every stage of training, for very little task success.
Whether a state is safe cannot be decided from the state alone. It depends on what the robot does next and on how much time remains to do it. Between continuing the maneuver and falling, VAPS places a third option that prior work usually omits: aborting the maneuver while staying upright.
